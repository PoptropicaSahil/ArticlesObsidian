## Clustering & K-Means (Lloyd's Algorithm)

PCA ⇒ finds a linear subspace <br>
Clustering ⇒ finds **discrete groupings** in the data (structure that persists even after projection)

### Setup

Data $x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \mathbb{R}^d$ ⇒ partition into $K$ clusters using **cluster indicators** $z_i \in \{1, \ldots, K\}$

Full assignment $\mathbf{z} = (z_1, \ldots, z_n)$ ⇒ $K^n$ possible assignments ⇒ exponential ⇒ no exhaustive search (the problem is NP-hard)

### Objective

For a given $\mathbf{z}$, cluster means

$\mu_k = \dfrac{\sum_{i=1}^n x_i \cdot \mathbf{1}[z_i = k]}{\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{1}[z_i = k]}$ (average of the points currently in cluster $k$)

Minimise the **within-cluster sum of squared distances**

$\min_{\mathbf{z}} \sum_{i=1}^n \Vert{}x_i - \mu_{z_i}\Vert{}^2$

> $\mu_k$ is not a free variable, it is *determined by* $\mathbf{z}$. The only thing we choose is the labels

---

### Lloyd's Algorithm

1. **Initialise** $z_i^{(0)}$ arbitrarily
2. **Compute means** $\mu_k^{(t)}$ of every cluster
3. **Reassign** every point to its nearest mean

$z_i^{(t+1)} = \arg\min_k \Vert{}x_i - \mu_k^{(t)}\Vert{}^2$

4. Repeat until no point changes cluster (**convergence**)

> [!NOTE] Pattern to remember (EM will reuse it)
> Step 2 = "given the labels, find the best means" <br>
> Step 3 = "given the means, find the best labels" <br>
> Each half is easy alone. Alternate between them

**Questions to answer**

1. Does it converge?
2. Is the result optimal?
3. What shape are the clusters?
4. How to initialise?
5. How to choose $K$?

---

### 1. Convergence

**Fact 1:** for any points $x_1, \ldots, x_l \in \mathbb{R}^d$

$\arg\min_v \sum_{i=1}^l \Vert{}x_i - v\Vert{}^2 = \dfrac{1}{l}\sum_{i=1}^l x_i$ (the mean)

⇒ the mean is the best single centre for a set of points *(proof: Golden Algebra 2, §1)*

**Claim:** $F(\mathbf{z}) = \sum_i \Vert{}x_i - \mu_{z_i}\Vert{}^2$ strictly decreases whenever a reassignment happens

$\underbrace{F(\mathbf{z}^{(t+1)})}_{\text{new labels, new means}} \;\leq\; \underbrace{\sum_i \Vert{}x_i - \mu^{(t)}_{z_i^{(t+1)}}\Vert{}^2}_{\text{new labels, old means}} \;<\; \underbrace{F(\mathbf{z}^{(t)})}_{\text{old labels, old means}}$

- **Right ($<$):** a point only moves if it finds a *strictly* closer mean ⇒ every move lowers its own term ⇒ strict
- **Left ($\leq$):** for each cluster, its new mean is the best centre for its new points (Fact 1) ⇒ can't be worse than the old mean

⇒ $F$ strictly decreases ⇒ the same partition never repeats ⇒ only finitely many ($\leq K^n$) ⇒ the algorithm **must terminate** $\square$

> Caveats <br>
> - Says nothing about *how many* iterations (up to $K^n$ in theory, fast in practice) <br>
> - Says nothing about reaching the **global** minimum, only a **local** one

### 2. Optimal?

No guarantee. Converges to a **local minimum** ⇒ result depends on initialisation

### 3. Nature of clusters: Voronoi regions

At convergence every point $x$ in cluster $k$ satisfies

$\Vert{}x - \mu_k\Vert{}^2 \leq \Vert{}x - \mu_j\Vert{}^2 \quad \forall j \neq k$

Expand, the $\Vert{}x\Vert{}^2$ terms cancel *(Golden Algebra 2, §2)*

$x^\top(\mu_j - \mu_k) \leq \dfrac{\Vert{}\mu_j\Vert{}^2 - \Vert{}\mu_k\Vert{}^2}{2}$

**Linear** inequality in $x$ ⇒ each cluster = intersection of $K-1$ half-spaces ⇒ exactly the **Voronoi regions** of $\{\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_K\}$, separated by perpendicular bisectors between pairs of means

![](Pasted%20image%2020261109150206.png)

> [!NOTE] Implication
> K-means clusters are always **convex** (and roughly spherical, a consequence of $\ell_2$ distance) <br>
> ⇒ it **cannot** find non-convex structure. Concentric rings get cut by a line/hyperplane, never recovered as inner vs outer

**Fix: Kernel K-means.** Map data to a high-dim feature space $\phi(x)$ (kernel trick, same as kernel PCA) where the natural clusters become linearly separable, then run Lloyd's there. Related to spectral clustering and eigenvectors of the kernel matrix

---

### 4. Initialisation

**Option 1 — Random uniform:** random cluster for every point, or pick $K$ random data points as the initial means. Simple, fast ⇒ run several times, keep the best result

**Option 2 — K-means++** (more principled): pick means one at a time, spread out

1. Choose $\mu_1^{(0)}$ uniformly at random from $\{x_1, \ldots, x_n\}$
2. For $l = 2, \ldots, K$: give every remaining point a score

$s(x) = \min_{j=1}^{l-1} \Vert{}x - \mu_j^{(0)}\Vert{}^2$ (squared distance to the nearest mean chosen so far)

Sample the next mean with probability proportional to the score

$P(\text{pick } x) = \dfrac{s(x)}{\sum_{x'} s(x')}$

> Far from all chosen means ⇒ high score ⇒ likely to be picked ⇒ means spread out across the data

**Why random, not "always take the farthest point"?** Deterministic farthest-point can be fooled by adversarial datasets (e.g. a lone outlier is always picked). The randomness gives an approximation guarantee

$\mathbb{E}\left[\sum_{i=1}^n \Vert{}x_i - \mu_{z_i}\Vert{}^2\right] \leq C \cdot \min_{\mathbf{z}} \sum_{i=1}^n \Vert{}x_i - \mu_{z_i}\Vert{}^2$

⇒ K-means++ is within a factor $C$ of optimal **in expectation**. No such guarantee for random uniform init

> Lecture says $C$ is "a constant". The known result is $C = O(\log K)$, i.e. it grows slowly with $K$

**Trade-off:** K-means++ needs $K$ passes over the data (scores recomputed every round) ⇒ slower than random init, matters at scale

### 5. Choosing $K$

$K = n$ gives objective $= 0$ (every point its own cluster) ⇒ minimising the objective alone is useless for choosing $K$ ⇒ **penalise large $K$**

$K^* = \arg\min_K \big[F(K) + \text{penalty}(K)\big]$, where $F(K)$ = best objective achieved with that $K$

| Criterion | Formula (smaller is better) |
|---|---|
| AIC (Akaike) | $2K - 2\log\mathcal{L}$ |
| BIC (Bayesian) | $K\log n - 2\log\mathcal{L}$ |
| Elbow method | Kink in the $F(K)$ vs $K$ curve |

- Here "$K$" in AIC/BIC = number of free parameters (grows with the number of clusters)
- AIC penalty is linear; BIC penalty is heavier for large $n$ ($K\log n$)
- Both need a likelihood $\mathcal{L}$ ⇒ need a probabilistic model of the data (coming next)
- Elbow = pure heuristic: plot $F(K)$ vs $K$, pick where diminishing returns start

### Summary: Lloyd's

| Question | Answer |
|---|---|
| Converge? | Yes. Objective strictly decreases per reassignment, finitely many partitions |
| Optimal? | No. Local minimum only |
| Cluster shape? | Voronoi ⇒ convex. Fails on non-convex ⇒ kernel K-means |
| Initialisation? | K-means++ (spread-out means, approximation guarantee in expectation) |
| Choice of $K$? | Minimise $F(K) + \text{penalty}(K)$ ⇒ AIC / BIC / elbow |

## Estimation & Maximum Likelihood

PCA, K-means ⇒ no probabilistic assumptions. Now: assume data comes from a **model** (a story of how data got made) with **unknown parameters** $\theta$ (the knobs of the story). Goal: estimate $\theta$ from the data

### Setup

**Example:** biased coin, unknown $P(\text{heads}) = p$. Observe $x_1, \ldots, x_n \in \{0, 1\}$

Two standard assumptions: **i.i.d.**

- **Independent:** $P(x_i \mid x_j) = P(x_i)$ ⇒ one outcome tells nothing about another
- **Identically distributed:** same $p$ for every trial (same "box" each time)

Under i.i.d. any $p \in (0, 1)$ *could* have produced the data ($p = 0$ or $1$ only ruled out if both heads and tails appear). So how to pick a principled estimate?

### Likelihood

**Fisher's principle of maximum likelihood:** pick the $p$ that makes the observed data **most probable**

Discrete case

$\mathcal{L}(p \mid x_1, \ldots, x_n) = P(x_1, \ldots, x_n \mid p) \stackrel{\text{i.i.d.}}{=} \prod_{i=1}^n p^{x_i}(1-p)^{1-x_i}$

Continuous case ⇒ single points have probability $0$ ⇒ use the PDF $f$

$\mathcal{L}(\theta \mid x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \prod_{i=1}^n f(x_i \mid \theta)$

> Same formula as a probability, different viewpoint <br>
> Probability: $\theta$ fixed, data varies <br>
> Likelihood: **data fixed, $\theta$ varies**. It is a function of $\theta$ and does not have to integrate to 1 over $\theta$

### Maximum Likelihood Estimator (MLE)

$\hat{\theta}_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_\theta \mathcal{L}(\theta \mid x_1, \ldots, x_n)$

Products are painful ⇒ take $\log$ (monotone ⇒ same maximiser)

$\hat{\theta}_{\text{ML}} = \arg\max_\theta \log\mathcal{L} = \arg\max_\theta \sum_{i=1}^n \log f(x_i \mid \theta)$

**Example 1 — Bernoulli**

$\log\mathcal{L}(p) = \sum_{i=1}^n \left[x_i\log p + (1 - x_i)\log(1-p)\right]$

Set $\dfrac{d}{dp}\log\mathcal{L} = 0$ ⇒ $\hat{p}_{\text{ML}} = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n x_i$ = fraction of heads ✓ matches intuition *(Golden Algebra 2, §3)*

![](Pasted%20image%2020261109150429.png)

Data $(1, 0, 1, 1)$ ⇒ $\mathcal{L}(p) = p^3(1-p)$ peaks at $p = 0.75$ = fraction of ones

**Example 2 — Gaussian** (known $\sigma^2$, unknown $\mu$)

$\log\mathcal{L}(\mu) = \text{const} - \sum_{i=1}^n \dfrac{(x_i - \mu)^2}{2\sigma^2}$

Maximise $\log\mathcal{L}$ ≡ minimise $\sum_i (x_i - \mu)^2$ ⇒ $\hat{\mu}_{\text{ML}} = \dfrac{1}{n}\sum_i x_i$ = sample mean *(Golden Algebra 2, §4)*

> Data Laplacian (sharper peak than Gaussian) ⇒ MLE = **sample median**, not mean *(Golden Algebra 2, §5)* <br>
> **The estimator depends on the assumed model**

### Summary

| Model | Unknown $\theta$ | MLE |
|---|---|---|
| Bernoulli | $p$ | fraction of 1s |
| Gaussian (known $\sigma^2$) | $\mu$ | sample mean |
| Gaussian (both unknown) | $\mu, \sigma^2$ | sample mean + sample variance (divide by $n$) |
| Laplacian | location | sample median |

**Recipe:** write the model's PDF/PMF ⇒ form the log-likelihood ⇒ maximise. Quality depends on whether the model assumption is reasonable

Next: what if we have **prior information** about $\theta$? ⇒ Bayesian estimation (MAP)

---

## Bayesian Estimation

MLE uses only the data. Often you also have a **prior belief** about $\theta$ before seeing data. Bayesian view ⇒ put a probability distribution on $\theta$

- **Prior** $p(\theta)$ = belief about $\theta$ before data
- **Posterior** $p(\theta \mid x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ = updated belief after data
- Bridge = **Bayes' theorem**

$\underbrace{p(\theta \mid \mathbf{x})}_{\text{posterior}} \propto \underbrace{p(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta)}_{\text{likelihood}} \cdot \underbrace{p(\theta)}_{\text{prior}}$

Denominator $p(\mathbf{x})$ (evidence) doesn't depend on $\theta$ ⇒ drop it, numerator is enough

**MAP** (maximum a posteriori) = most probable $\theta$ under the posterior

$\hat{\theta}_{\text{MAP}} = \arg\max_\theta \left[\log p(\mathbf{x} \mid \theta) + \log p(\theta)\right]$ ⇒ MLE + a log-prior bonus

### Example: Bernoulli + Beta prior

Prior must live on $[0, 1]$ ⇒ natural choice: **Beta distribution**

$p \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha, \beta)$, i.e. density $\dfrac{1}{Z}\,p^{\alpha-1}(1-p)^{\beta-1}$, $p \in [0, 1]$

$\alpha, \beta > 0$ = **hyperparameters** (you set them to encode your belief)

![](Pasted%20image%2020261109150631.png)

Posterior (Bernoulli likelihood × Beta prior) is **also Beta** ⇒ **conjugate prior** *(Golden Algebra 2, §6)*

$p \mid \mathbf{x} \sim \text{Beta}(\alpha + n_H,\ \beta + n_T)$

$n_H$ = number of heads, $n_T$ = number of tails

> Prior = **ghost data**: $\alpha + \beta$ pseudo-observations ($\alpha$ imaginary heads, $\beta$ imaginary tails) added to the real ones

| Estimator | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| MLE (no prior) | $\dfrac{n_H}{n}$ | |
| MAP (mode of posterior) | $\dfrac{\alpha + n_H - 1}{\alpha + \beta + n - 2}$ | $= $ MLE if $\alpha = \beta = 1$ (uniform prior) |
| Posterior mean | $\dfrac{\alpha + n_H}{\alpha + \beta + n}$ | $=$ MLE if $\alpha = \beta = 0$. Weighted average of prior mean and MLE ⇒ **shrinks MLE toward the prior** |

$n \to \infty$ ⇒ data overwhelms the prior ⇒ MAP and posterior mean $\to$ MLE

**Conjugacy** (here Beta–Bernoulli) is special: prior and posterior have the same form ⇒ just update the parameters ($\alpha \to \alpha + n_H$, $\beta \to \beta + n_T$), no need to recompute the distribution

## Gaussian Mixture Models (GMM)

A single Gaussian can't model **multimodal** data (several bumps) ⇒ use a **mixture of Gaussians**

> [!NOTE] What does "latent" mean? <br>
> Picture heights of men and women pooled into one histogram ⇒ two bumps. Every person has a hidden label "group $=$ man or woman" that is **not recorded**. That hidden label is the **latent variable** $z_i$ <br>
> **Generative story** = a recipe for how the data was made: (1) secretly pick a group, (2) draw a number from that group's bell curve <br>
> **Learning** = run the recipe backwards. From the numbers alone, recover (a) the bell curves and (b) which group each point probably came from

### Generative story (2 steps per data point)

**Step 1 — pick a component**

$z_i \sim \text{Categorical}(\pi_1, \ldots, \pi_K), \quad \pi_k \geq 0,\ \sum_k \pi_k = 1$

($\pi_k$ = probability that a point comes from component $k$)

**Step 2 — sample from that component's Gaussian**

$x_i \mid z_i = k \sim \mathcal{N}(\mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$

$z_i$ is **latent** (unobserved) ⇒ GMM is a **latent variable model**

![](Pasted%20image%2020261109150708.png)

> Shown in 1D. In $\mathbb{R}^d$ each $\sigma_k^2$ becomes a covariance matrix $\Sigma_k$ ⇒ this is what allows *elliptical* clusters

### Parameters to estimate

$\underbrace{\pi_1, \ldots, \pi_K}_{\text{mixing weights}},\quad \underbrace{\mu_1, \ldots, \mu_K}_{\text{means}},\quad \underbrace{\sigma_1^2, \ldots, \sigma_K^2}_{\text{variances}}$

$\sim 3K$ parameters ($3K - 1$ since $\sum_k \pi_k = 1$). Unlike the single Gaussian, no obvious closed-form guess ⇒ apply MLE ⇒ leads to the **EM algorithm**

---

## GMM: Maximum Likelihood

### Likelihood

i.i.d. + two-step story, **marginalise out** the latent $z_i$ (sum over all the groups the point could have come from)

$P(x_i) = \sum_k P(z_i = k)\,P(x_i \mid z_i = k) = \sum_k \pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)$

$\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \prod_{i=1}^n \sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k \cdot \underbrace{\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}\,\sigma_k}\exp\left(-\dfrac{(x_i - \mu_k)^2}{2\sigma_k^2}\right)}_{\mathcal{N}(x_i;\,\mu_k,\,\sigma_k^2)}$

$\log\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_{i=1}^n \log\left(\sum_{k=1}^K \pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)\right)$

> [!NOTE] The problem
> $\log(\sum)$ ⇒ can't split into a clean sum of logs <br>
> Derivative $= 0$ has no closed form <br>
> $\pi_k$ are constrained ($\sum_k \pi_k = 1$) <br>
> ⇒ need a smarter approach that uses the latent structure

### Detour: Convexity & Jensen's inequality

$f$ is **convex** if for all $a, b$ and $\lambda \in [0, 1]$

$f(\lambda a + (1-\lambda)b) \leq \lambda f(a) + (1-\lambda)f(b)$ (chord lies above the curve)

**Concave** = inequality reversed (chord below the curve). Linear functions are both

![](Pasted%20image%2020261109150802.png)

**Jensen's inequality** = same thing for any weighted average. For **concave** $f$, weights $\lambda_k \geq 0$, $\sum_k \lambda_k = 1$ *(proof by induction: Golden Algebra 2, §7)*

$f\left(\sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k a_k\right) \geq \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k f(a_k)$

Convex $f$ ⇒ inequality flips

**Key fact:** $\log$ is concave ($\log'' x = -1/x^2 < 0$) ⇒

$\log\left(\sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k a_k\right) \geq \sum_{k=1}^K \lambda_k \log a_k$

> Moves the $\sum$ *outside* the $\log$ at the cost of an inequality (a **lower bound**). EM = repeatedly tighten and climb this bound

---

## The EM Algorithm for GMMs

### The key trick: introduce $\lambda$

Stuck on $\log\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_i \log\left(\sum_k \pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)\right)$

Introduce auxiliary weights $\lambda_{ik} > 0$, $\sum_k \lambda_{ik} = 1$ (for each point $i$). Multiply and divide inside the sum (changes nothing)

$\log\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \sum_i \log\sum_k \lambda_{ik} \cdot \dfrac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)}{\lambda_{ik}}$

Jensen ($\log$ concave, $\lambda_{ik}$ = the weights)

$\log\mathcal{L}(\theta) \geq \underbrace{\sum_i\sum_k \lambda_{ik}\log\dfrac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)}{\lambda_{ik}}}_{\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta,\ \lambda)\ \text{— modified log-likelihood}}$

- Holds for **any** valid $\lambda$
- $\log(\sum)$ is gone ⇒ replaced by $\sum\log$, which is easy

> [!NOTE] Key insight
> Fix $\lambda$ ⇒ easy to maximise over $\theta$ <br>
> Fix $\theta$ ⇒ easy to maximise over $\lambda$ <br>
> **Alternate between the two**

> [!NOTE] Intuition (chicken and egg)
> If we **knew the hidden labels** $z_i$ ⇒ easy: for every group take its mean, variance and fraction (ordinary MLE) <br>
> If we **knew the parameters** $\theta$ ⇒ easy: for every point ask "how likely is it from group $k$?" (Bayes) <br>
> We know neither ⇒ guess one, compute the other, repeat <br>
> $\lambda_{ik}$ = our **soft guess** of the hidden label: "point $i$ belongs to group $k$ with degree $\lambda_{ik}$" (rows sum to 1) <br>
> Picture: $\tilde{\mathcal{L}}$ is a **floor** under the true $\log\mathcal{L}$. E-step lifts the floor until it **touches** the curve at the current $\theta$. M-step **climbs** the floor. The curve is above the floor ⇒ it rises at least as much

---

### M-step (fix $\lambda$, maximise over $\theta$)

$\hat{\mu}_k = \dfrac{\sum_i \lambda_{ik}\,x_i}{\sum_i \lambda_{ik}}, \qquad \hat{\sigma}_k^2 = \dfrac{\sum_i \lambda_{ik}(x_i - \hat{\mu}_k)^2}{\sum_i \lambda_{ik}}, \qquad \hat{\pi}_k = \dfrac{\sum_i \lambda_{ik}}{n}$

Weighted mean, weighted variance, weighted fraction ⇒ $\lambda_{ik}$ = weight of point $i$ in cluster $k$ *(derivation: Golden Algebra 2, §8b)*

> If every $\lambda_{ik}$ is $0$ or $1$ (hard labels) these are exactly K-means' cluster mean, variance and fraction

### E-step (fix $\theta$, maximise over $\lambda$)

Lagrange multipliers (+ Bayes) *(Golden Algebra 2, §8c)*

$\hat{\lambda}_{ik} = P(z_i = k \mid x_i) = \dfrac{\pi_k\,\mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_k, \sigma_k^2)}{\sum_{l=1}^K \pi_l\,\mathcal{N}(x_i; \mu_l, \sigma_l^2)}$

Just Bayes' theorem: given the parameters, probability that point $i$ came from cluster $k$ (called the **responsibility** of cluster $k$ for point $i$)

> Plugging $\hat{\lambda}$ back makes $\tilde{\mathcal{L}}(\theta, \hat{\lambda}) = \log\mathcal{L}(\theta)$ exactly ⇒ bound is **tight** at the current $\theta$

### Mini example (1D, $K = 2$)

**E-step:** $\pi = (0.5, 0.5)$, $\mu = (0, 4)$, $\sigma^2 = (1, 1)$, point $x = 1$

$\mathcal{N}(1; 0, 1) \approx 0.2420$, $\mathcal{N}(1; 4, 1) \approx 0.0044$

$\lambda_{i1} = \dfrac{0.5 \cdot 0.2420}{0.5 \cdot 0.2420 + 0.5 \cdot 0.0044} \approx 0.98$, $\quad \lambda_{i2} \approx 0.02$

⇒ $x = 1$ is much closer to the first bump ⇒ 98% cluster 1

**M-step:** points $0, 1, 5$ with weights

| point | $\lambda_{i1}$ | $\lambda_{i2}$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 1 | 0 |
| 1 | 0.9 | 0.1 |
| 5 | 0.1 | 0.9 |

$\hat{\mu}_1 = \dfrac{1 \cdot 0 + 0.9 \cdot 1 + 0.1 \cdot 5}{1 + 0.9 + 0.1} = 0.7$

$\hat{\sigma}_1^2 = \dfrac{1(0.7)^2 + 0.9(0.3)^2 + 0.1(4.3)^2}{2} = 1.21$

$\hat{\pi}_1 = \dfrac{2}{3}$

Similarly $\hat{\mu}_2 = \dfrac{0 + 0.1 \cdot 1 + 0.9 \cdot 5}{1.0} = 4.6$, $\hat{\pi}_2 = \dfrac{1}{3}$

---

### The EM Algorithm

1. **Initialise** $\theta^{(0)} = (\pi, \mu, \sigma^2)$
2. **E-step:** with $\theta^{(t)}$ fixed, compute all $\lambda_{ik}^{(t)}$ (formula above)
3. **M-step:** with $\lambda^{(t)}$ fixed, update $\theta^{(t+1)}$ (weighted formulas above)
4. Repeat 2–3 until convergence (log-likelihood stops improving)

### EM vs Lloyd's

| | Lloyd's (K-means) | EM (GMM) |
|---|---|---|
| Assignment | Hard ($z_i \in \{1, \ldots, K\}$) | Soft ($\lambda_{ik} \in [0, 1]$) |
| Parameters estimated | Means only | Means + variances + mixing weights |
| Cluster shape | Voronoi (spherical) | Elliptical (captures covariance structure) |
| Objective | Sum of squared distances | Log-likelihood of the GMM |
| Convergence | Local minimum of the objective | Local maximum of the log-likelihood |

EM gives **soft clustering** ⇒ $\lambda_{ik}$ = posterior probability that point $i$ belongs to cluster $k$. Hard clustering: $z_i = \arg\max_k \lambda_{ik}$

### Convergence guarantee

EM converges to a **local maximum** of $\log\mathcal{L}(\theta)$, not necessarily global

- Each E-step makes the bound tight at the current $\theta$
- Each M-step improves $\theta$ against that bound
- ⇒ log-likelihood is **non-decreasing** at every iteration *(proof: Golden Algebra 2, §8d)*

> **Initialisation matters.** Common practice: run Lloyd's first, use its hard-clustering means / variances / fractions as $\theta^{(0)}$
